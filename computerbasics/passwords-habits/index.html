Passwords: habits that keep your accounts safer

A password is the key to an account. If someone else gets the key, they can enter. These habits make that much harder.

Make it long

A long password is stronger than a short one with strange signs. Four or five unrelated words put together, such as "teacup river lantern monsoon", are strong and easier to remember than "P@ss1". Do not use your name, birthday, phone number or the word "password".

Use a different one for important accounts

If you use the same password everywhere and one website is broken into, the thieves try that password on your email and bank. Your email matters most: whoever controls it can reset your other passwords. Give your email its own strong password.

Where to keep them

You cannot remember dozens of long passwords, so you need a safe place. Some people use a password manager, a program made for this. Others keep a written list at home, away from the computer and out of sight of visitors. Both are better than using one password for everything. Never keep the list in a note called "passwords" on a phone that others can pick up, and never send it to anyone.

Two-step sign-in

Many services can ask for a second proof after your password, such as a code sent to your phone. If it is offered for your email and your money apps, switch it on. It means that the password alone is not enough.

One-time codes are secret. If a person, a caller or a message asks you to read out or send a code that has just arrived on your phone, stop. A genuine service never needs you to tell the code to a person.

If you think a password is known

Change it at once, using a device you trust, and change it anywhere else you used it. Then look at the recent activity of the account.
