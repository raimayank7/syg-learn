How to learn formulas

A formula read is forgotten in a day; a formula used five times stays. For each line above, cover the example, make up your own numbers and work it. Then do it backwards: given the NAV and the units, find the amount; given the break-even and the strike, find the premium. Examination questions often turn a formula round in this way, and a candidate who has only memorised its usual direction is stuck.

Say what each formula means in words. "CAGR is the yearly rate which, compounded, takes the beginning value to the end value" is more useful than the symbols, because it tells you when the measure applies and what it hides. "Basis is spot minus futures, and it shrinks to nothing at expiry" answers three different questions.

Units and signs

  • Returns are percentages; ratios such as P/E and interest coverage are "times"; payoffs and profits are rupees per share until multiplied by the lot size.
  • A negative profit is a loss. Keep the sign through the calculation and interpret it at the end.
  • Convert the period before using a rate: an annual rate for one month is multiplied by 1/12.
  • Round only at the last step.

What is not here

This sheet has no tax rates, expense limits, margin percentages, lot sizes or other figures set by the regulator or the exchanges. They change, and a sheet that states them is soon wrong. Take them from the current NISM workbook. The examples use round, made-up numbers.

Questions candidates ask

Is a formula sheet given in the NISM exam?

We did not find a statement about this on the pages we read. Assume you must know the formulas, and check NISM's candidate instructions.

Which formulas matter most for the mutual fund exam?

NAV, units allotted, exit load, absolute return, annualised return and CAGR.

Which formulas matter most for equity derivatives?

Futures profit and loss, cost of carry pricing, basis, option payoff, break-even, intrinsic value and time value.
