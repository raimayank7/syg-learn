Leverage and efficiency ratios: debt, cover and days

Leverage ratios show how much a business relies on borrowed money. Efficiency ratios show how fast it turns stock and sales into cash.

RatioHow it is worked outSample result
Debt to equityborrowings / shareholders' equity0.79 times
Debt ratiototal liabilities / total assets0.58 times

Debt to equity

How much borrowing there is for each unit of the owners' money.

Debt ratio

The share of the assets that is financed by all liabilities, not only borrowings.

Interest cover

How many times the operating profit covers the interest. A bigger number means a bigger cushion.

Inventory turnover

How many times the stock is sold and replaced in the period, measured at cost.

Inventory days

About how many days of sales at cost the stock on hand would last.

Receivable days

About how many days of sales are waiting to be paid by customers.

Payable days

About how many days of costs are waiting to be paid to suppliers.

Asset turnover

How many units of sales each unit of assets produces in the period.

The sample figures (in any one currency) are: revenue 1,200,000, cost of goods sold 780,000, operating profit 150,000, interest 30,000, net profit 90,000, cash 60,000, receivables 150,000, inventory 140,000, current assets 380,000, total assets 900,000, payables 110,000, current liabilities 250,000, total liabilities 520,000, equity 380,000, borrowings 300,000. They are made up.

Using them

  • Days measures depend on the period of the figures: if revenue is for a year, 365 is right; for a quarter, they would need changing.
  • Read receivable days next to payable days: a business that waits longer to be paid than it takes to pay needs money to bridge the gap.

These are common textbook definitions, using the closing figures you type and 365 days a year. Books and companies differ (some use average balances), so use the definition your course or lender uses. We give no target values and no advice on any business.
