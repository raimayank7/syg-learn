ipconfig, ping and tracert: check your network

When the internet "is not working", three commands find out where the trouble lies: at your computer, at your router, or beyond it. They only look and measure. They change nothing, apart from two ipconfig options described below.

These commands were not run for this page, because their output would show a real network. The sample outputs are illustrations with made-up addresses.

ipconfig: your computer's addresses

The lines that matter:

  • IPv4 Address is your computer's address on the local network.
  • Default Gateway is the address of your router, the door to the internet.

Addresses beginning 192.168, 10 or 172.16 to 172.31 are private ones, used inside homes and offices. The address the rest of the internet sees belongs to your router and is different.

If the IPv4 address begins with 169.254, the computer asked the router for an address and got no reply. That points to the Wi-Fi or cable connection, or the router itself.

ipconfig /all adds more detail, including the adapter's physical (MAC) address and the DNS servers.

Renewing the address

  • /release then /renew gives up the current address and asks the router for a fresh one. It often cures a connection that says "No internet" after the laptop wakes from sleep. The connection drops for a few seconds while it runs.
  • /flushdns empties the computer's memory of website addresses. It helps when one site will not open although others do, or after a site has moved.

ping: is it reachable?

ping sends a small message to another computer and times the reply.

Each "Reply from" line is one message that came back, with the time it took in thousandths of a second. The summary gives the number lost.

You seeIt usually means
replies with low timesthe connection to that device is good
replies with high or uneven timesa slow or congested link
"Request timed out"no answer: the device is off, unreachable, or set to ignore pings
"Destination host unreachable"your computer or router has no route to it
"could not find host"the name could not be turned into an address: a DNS problem

ping -t keeps going until you press Ctrl + C, which is useful for watching a connection that drops now and then. ping -n 10 sends ten messages.

A three-step check

Work outwards from your own computer.

  1. Ping your router, using the Default Gateway address from ipconfig. No reply means the trouble is between your computer and the router: Wi-Fi, cable or the router.
  2. Ping a public address by number, such as a well-known public DNS server. Replies here, with none in step 3, mean the internet connection works and name lookup does not.
  3. Ping a website by name, such as ping example.com. Replies mean everything works, and the problem is with one site or one program.

Remember that some servers are set not to answer pings, so a single timeout proves little. Try a second address.

tracert: where does it stop?

tracert lists each router on the way to a destination, with the time to reach it.

Each numbered line is one hop. Rows of stars mean a router that did not answer, which is common and not a fault in itself. What matters is where the replies stop for good, or where the times jump sharply. A problem at the first hop is in your own network. A problem several hops out is with your internet provider or beyond, and is something to report to them, not something you can fix.

nslookup: names into addresses

It asks your DNS server for the address behind a name. If this fails while pinging a numeric address works, the DNS server is the problem.

Related commands

  • hostname prints your computer's name.
  • getmac lists the physical addresses of the network adapters.
  • netstat -an lists the network connections open on your computer.

A word of caution

Network commands found on websites are sometimes aimed at other people's computers or networks. Testing your own connection is fine. Probing systems that you do not own or manage may be against the law and against your provider's terms.
