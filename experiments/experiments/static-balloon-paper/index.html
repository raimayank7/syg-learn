Static Balloon and Paper: a safe home experiment

Can a rubbed balloon pick up bits of paper without touching them?

Adult supervision needed. An adult must be nearby for every experiment on this site, from start to finish. Children should never do the steps marked Adult alone. If anything feels unsafe, stop.

What you need

Tick each thing as you find it. You can print this page.

  • balloon
  • small pieces of paper
  • clean dry hair or a woollen cloth

Predict first

Before you start, write down what you think will happen. Then you can see whether you were right. Scientists do this too.

Steps

  1. Tear a sheet of paper into very small pieces, about the size of a fingernail, and spread them on a table.
  2. AdultAn adult blows up the balloon and ties it. Young children should not blow up balloons, and a broken balloon should never go near a mouth.
  3. Rub the balloon on clean dry hair or a woollen cloth about twenty times.
  4. Slowly bring the balloon close to the paper pieces, but do not touch them. What do they do?
  5. Rub the balloon again and try it on a thin stream of water from a tap, held a little way away.
Safety. Keep balloon pieces away from small children, because they can choke on them. This is a tiny, harmless static charge. Never try to copy it with sockets, plugs or wires.

Stop if…

  • Stop if a balloon bursts and tell an adult. Pick up every piece.
  • Stop if the air is very damp and nothing happens. The experiment works best on dry days.

Why it works

Rubbing moves tiny charged particles from one thing to the other. The balloon ends up with a charge, and a charged thing can attract light things such as paper bits. This charge on a thing that stays in one place is called static electricity.

Try next

Does a bigger balloon work better? Does rubbing more times change how many pieces it lifts? Count them.
