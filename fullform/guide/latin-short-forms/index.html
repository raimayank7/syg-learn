Latin short forms used in English

Some of the most common short forms in English are not English at all. They came from Latin, the language of learning in Europe for many centuries, and one well-known one came from French.

Because the words behind them are foreign, people use these every day without knowing what the letters stand for, and that leads to mistakes. Once you know that "e.g." means "for example" and "i.e." means "that is", you will not confuse them again.

e.g. and i.e.

Use e.g. when you give one or more examples out of many: "Take citrus fruit, e.g. oranges and lemons." Other citrus fruits exist. Use i.e. when you say the same thing again more exactly: "The exam is in the last week, i.e. from the 24th." Nothing else is meant. A memory aid: e.g. = "example given", i.e. = "in essence". These are aids, not the real words.

etc.

"Etc." means "and the rest". Do not write "and etc.", because the "and" is already inside it. Do not use it after "e.g.", because a list of examples is already incomplete. And avoid it for people; "and others" is politer.

More from Latin

  • et al.: et alii, "and others", used after the first author of a book or paper.
  • viz.: videlicet, "namely".
  • cf.: confer, "compare".
  • ibid.: ibidem, "in the same place", used in footnotes.
  • c. or ca.: circa, "about", before a date: c. 1500.
  • p.a.: per annum, "a year": 8 percent p.a.
  • a.m. and p.m.: see our page on AM and PM.
  • lb: libra, the Roman pound, which is why a pound in weight is written lb.
  • No.: numero, "by number", which is why "number" is shortened with an o.
  • Rx: on a prescription, from recipe, "take".
  • QED: quod erat demonstrandum, "which was to be shown", written at the end of a proof.

Punctuation

In British and Indian usage, "e.g." and "i.e." are written with full stops and usually without a comma after them. "Etc." takes one full stop; if it ends the sentence, do not add a second. "Vs" is also written "v." in the names of court cases.

In formal writing

These short forms are acceptable in notes, tables and brackets. In the running text of an essay or letter, the English words often read better: "for example", "that is", "and so on".
