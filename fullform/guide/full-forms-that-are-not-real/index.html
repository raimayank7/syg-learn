Full forms that are not real

Many "full forms" passed around in messages and even printed in guide books were made up long after the word existed. The words below are ordinary words, not abbreviations.

The invented expansions are called backronyms: someone takes an existing word and fits a phrase to its letters. They are harmless as a memory game. The trouble starts when they are taught as facts and turn up in quiz answers. A simple test exposes most of them: abbreviations made from initials became common only in the last hundred and fifty years or so, while words like news, police and tea are many centuries old. A word older than the habit of making initials cannot have begun as initials.

WordThe invented full formWhere the word really comes from
NEWSNorth, East, West, SouthThe word comes from "new": news is new information. It has been used in English since the Middle Ages, long before the idea of initials.
POLICEPublic Officer for Legal Investigations and Criminal EmergenciesThe word came into English from French, and goes back to the Greek "polis", city, the same root as "policy" and "politics".
COMPUTERCommonly Operated Machine Particularly Used for Technical and Educational ResearchA computer is something that computes. The word was first used for people who did calculations.
CHESSChariot, Horse, Elephant, SoldiersThe word comes through Old French from the Persian "shah", king, the same root as "check".
TEATaste and Energy AdmittedThe word comes from "te" in the Min Chinese of the Fujian coast, carried to Europe by Dutch traders. "Chai" comes from the Mandarin "cha".
INDIAIndependent Nation Declared In AugustThe name comes from the river Indus, known to the Greeks and Persians thousands of years before 1947.
MATHSMentally Attempted Tough and Hard SumsShort for mathematics, from the Greek "mathema", something learned.
PENPower Enriched in NibFrom the Latin "penna", feather, because pens were once made from quills.
GOLFGentlemen Only, Ladies ForbiddenThe word is an old Scots one, probably related to a Dutch word for club.
WIFEWorries Invited For EverA joke. "Wife" is an Old English word that simply meant woman.
DATEDay And Time EvolutionFrom the Latin "data", given, used in the dating of Roman letters.
BYEBe With You Every time"Goodbye" is a shortening of "God be with ye".
SMILESweet Memories In Lips ExpressionAn ordinary English word of Scandinavian origin.
LOVELife's Only Valuable EmotionAn Old English word; the expansion is a greeting-card invention.

Names that look like abbreviations but are not

Some names are written in capitals or have a technical look, and people assume there must be a phrase behind them.

  • Wi-Fi: Wi-Fi is a brand name of the Wi-Fi Alliance for wireless networking. It is often expanded as "Wireless Fidelity", but that phrase was only an early advertising slogan.
  • FASTag: FASTag is the brand name of India's electronic toll tag. It is a name, not an abbreviation.
  • OK: The most widely accepted origin is "oll korrect", a joke spelling of "all correct" from Boston newspapers of 1839.
  • SOS: The international distress signal in Morse code. The letters were chosen because they are easy to send, not as initials; "Save Our Souls" was attached later.

Real ones that sound invented

The opposite also happens. LASER, RADAR, SONAR and SCUBA really are initials, and CAPTCHA really does stand for a long phrase, though it was built to resemble the word "capture". The entries for these are on the science and computer pages.

What to write in an examination

If a school paper asks for the "full form of computer" and your textbook gives the invented one, the marking scheme probably expects it; write what your book says, and know for yourself that it is not the origin of the word. In competitive examinations set by national bodies, such invented expansions are rarely asked, because the question setters know they are not real.

How to check a full form yourself

  1. Look at the organisation's own website or letterhead for the name it uses.
  2. For an ordinary word, look up its origin in a good dictionary. If the dictionary gives a history in Latin, Greek, French or Old English, it is not an abbreviation.
  3. Be suspicious of expansions that are jokes, compliments or moral lessons. Real names are usually dull.
