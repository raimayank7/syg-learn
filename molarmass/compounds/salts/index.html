Molar mass of salts

A salt is the ionic compound left when an acid is neutralised by a base. Salts have no separate molecules, so chemists speak of the mass of one formula unit.

Read the formula of a salt carefully before counting. Brackets with a number, as in aluminium sulphate or calcium phosphate, multiply everything inside. Many salts in the laboratory are sold as hydrates, so check the label of the bottle: the anhydrous salt on this page and its hydrate have different molar masses.

Which values? This site uses the abridged standard atomic weights published by IUPAC (CIAAW), read on 12 November 2026. Your textbook or question paper may round them differently, for example H = 1 and O = 16, so its answer can differ slightly. In an exam, use the values the paper gives.

Reading the range

The 46 salts on this page run from 41.99 to 461.00 g/mol, with a median of 136.21 g/mol. The heaviest, lead(II) iodide, is about 11.0 times the mass of the lightest, sodium fluoride. The three lightest are sodium fluoride, ammonium chloride, sodium chloride; the three heaviest are lead(II) iodide, iron(III) sulphate, aluminium sulphate. When you compare compounds of this kind, remember that a larger molar mass does not mean more atoms of an element: it is the sum of all the atoms in the formula, so a compound with one heavy atom can outweigh one with many light atoms.

To use a molar mass, divide the mass of a sample in grams by the molar mass to get moles, or multiply the moles by the molar mass to get grams. For example, 100 g of the lightest compound here is 2.38 mol and 100 g of the heaviest is 0.22 mol, so the same mass holds many more particles of the lighter one. That is why chemists weigh in grams but count in moles.
