Molar mass of organic compounds

Organic compounds are built on chains and rings of carbon atoms. Their formulas are longer, so a careful atom count matters most here.

Organic formulas are often written to show structure, as in CH3COOH or C2H5OH. Add up each element across the whole formula before you start. Different compounds can share one molecular formula (isomers) and so one molar mass, and many share an empirical formula: glucose, acetic acid, lactic acid and formaldehyde are all CH2O in the simplest ratio.

This page lists 25 organic compounds with their formulas and molar masses, lightest first. The lightest here is ethyne at 26.04 g/mol and the heaviest is sucrose at 342.30 g/mol. Tap a name for the full working, the percentage of each element and worked examples.

Which values? This site uses the abridged standard atomic weights published by IUPAC (CIAAW), read on 12 November 2026. Your textbook or question paper may round them differently, for example H = 1 and O = 16, so its answer can differ slightly. In an exam, use the values the paper gives.

Reading the range

The 25 organic compounds on this page run from 26.04 to 342.30 g/mol, with a median of 90.08 g/mol. The heaviest, sucrose, is about 13.1 times the mass of the lightest, ethyne. The three lightest are ethyne, ethene, formaldehyde; the three heaviest are sucrose, caffeine, aspirin. When you compare compounds of this kind, remember that a larger molar mass does not mean more atoms of an element: it is the sum of all the atoms in the formula, so a compound with one heavy atom can outweigh one with many light atoms.

To use a molar mass, divide the mass of a sample in grams by the molar mass to get moles, or multiply the moles by the molar mass to get grams. For example, 100 g of the lightest compound here is 3.84 mol and 100 g of the heaviest is 0.29 mol, so the same mass holds many more particles of the lighter one. That is why chemists weigh in grams but count in moles.
