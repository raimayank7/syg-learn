Common CUET UG mistakes and how to avoid them

These are mistakes that teachers see often in CUET candidates. They are our own observations, not from NTA, each with a fix.

Choosing mistakes

  • Choosing papers before checking the universities. Fix: start from the programme pages (see choosing subjects).
  • Taking extra papers by habit. Fix: each extra paper costs more and needs study time.
  • Ignoring the language paper. Fix: it is a reading test and rewards daily reading.

Studying mistakes

  • Reading without testing. Fix: do questions after each topic.
  • Neglecting the General Aptitude Test. Fix: 15 to 20 small questions every day.
  • Studying only the subject you love. Fix: keep a little time for every paper you chose.

Exam mistakes

  • Spending too long on one question. Fix: about 72 seconds each; mark and move on.
  • Guessing without a rule. Fix: decide it in advance.
  • Not checking the shift timings of each paper. Fix: read the admit card the night before.

Practical mistakes

  • Missing the application window. Fix: check the NTA site weekly from the new year.
  • Filling two forms. Fix: fill once, carefully; multiple forms lead to cancellation of the result.
  • Forgetting the admit card or ID. Fix: pack the night before.

See also the twelve-week plan.

How to keep a mistake from coming back

A mistake you only notice comes back. A mistake you write down, with its reason, comes back less often. After each timed paper, list the wrong answers in three columns: what the question asked, what you did, and what you should have done. Once a week, read the whole list and mark the kinds of mistake that appear more than once. Those are your real syllabus.

Some mistakes have mechanical cures: read the question twice, write the unit on every line, check the sign before moving on. Others, such as a half-learned topic, need time with the book. Tell the two kinds apart, and spend your time on the second kind.

A note on comparing yourself with others

CUET has many papers and every candidate takes a different set, so comparing your scores with a friend's tells you little. Compare your own papers over time. Each university also uses the scores in its own way, so a number that is good for one programme may not be the number another needs.
