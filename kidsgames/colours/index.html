Colour Match

Find the colour that is asked for, then name the colour of everyday things. Age 2 to 5

Loading the game...

How to play

  1. Read the question aloud, or press the read-aloud box on the start screen so the page reads it.
  2. The child taps one of the coloured circles.
  3. A right answer says the colour name back and moves on. A wrong tap fades that circle so the child can try again.
  4. After two wrong taps the right circle glows, so nobody is stuck.

A round has eight questions and no clock. The best stars for each level are kept on this device only.

Levels

  • Level 1: Four colours: red, blue, yellow and green.
  • Level 2: Eight colours: adds orange, purple, pink and brown.
  • Level 3: What colour is a banana? Matches things to eleven colours.

Start at level 1. Move up when most answers are right the first time.

What this game practises

Matching a colour to its name is the first step in sorting and describing the world. Children learn to see the colour first and attach the word second, so the game always shows the word in the question and says it again after a right answer. Level 3 moves from a bare colour to a colour belonging to a thing, such as a leaf or a lemon, which is how colour words are used in real talk.

Good to know

Most children can match two colours that look the same before they can say either name, and this is normal. Naming usually follows after many weeks of hearing the words in daily life. A game helps by giving a clear picture, a clear word and an answer that is checked at once, but it cannot replace a grown-up pointing at the red cup at breakfast.

Tips for grown-ups

  • Start with level 1 even if your child knows some colours. Four colours make a calm first game.
  • Say the colour name yourself after each answer: "Yes, red like the tomato."
  • If two colours get mixed up often, play with only those two on paper first.
  • Do not worry if a child calls every colour by the same name for a while. It is a stage.

Play it without a screen

Sort coloured things into bowls: socks, blocks, spoons, bangles, leaves. Say "Find me something green" on a walk or in the kitchen. A colour hunt around the house with a bag, where each colour in turn must be found and named, can fill a rainy afternoon.

A word of care

A few children cannot tell some reds and greens apart. If a child keeps missing the same pair even after plenty of play, mention it to a doctor or an eye-care worker at the next visit. This game is for play and cannot test eyesight.
