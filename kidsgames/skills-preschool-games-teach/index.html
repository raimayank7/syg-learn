Preschool Games

What preschool games teach

Preschool learning games can look like a heap of unrelated activities, but each one builds a particular skill. This page explains what the skills are, and which game helps with which one, so you can choose with a purpose.

Seeing and sorting

Looking at things and putting them into groups is the base of most learning. Colour Match and Shape Spotter train the eye to notice one feature. Big and Small trains comparing. Odd One Out trains sorting by kind.

Numbers

Counting needs saying the numbers in order and giving each thing one number. Count the Things trains the second part, and Number Friends trains knowing the written number and the order of numbers.

Letters and sounds

Letter Hunt teaches letter names, First Letter links the first sound in a word to its letter, and Hindi Letters does the same for the Hindi varnmala. Animal Sounds builds listening and speaking, and Opposites builds vocabulary.

Thinking and memory

What Comes Next asks the child to find a repeating rule, and Memory Match trains remembering where things are. These do not look like "school work" but are what later learning is built upon.

Movement and language matter most

None of this takes the place of running, climbing, talking and being read to. WHO recommends at least three hours of physical activity a day for children aged 1 to 4, and says reading and storytelling with a caregiver is very important. Games are an extra, not the main course.

A simple plan

  • Two or three games a week, a few minutes each.
  • The same game several times, until it feels easy, before moving up a level.
  • Always something real first: the object, the walk, the song. The game second.

How to tell a game is working

  • The child asks to play it again, and is happy when it ends.
  • Answers get right more often after a week or two at the same level.
  • The child talks about it afterwards: "I found the blue one."
  • The child uses the ideas away from the screen, pointing out circles on a plate or counting the steps.

If none of these happen after a few weeks, the game may be too hard, too easy or not to the child's taste. Change the level or put it aside. There is no need to stick to one game.

Common worries

"My child only taps without looking." That is common with very young children. Sit beside them, read the question aloud and point to the right part of the screen.

"My child wants to play only one game." This is fine. Repeating is how small children learn. Offer a second game from the same group (for example Shape Spotter after Colour Match) now and then.

"My child is behind other children." Children learn at very different speeds, and these games are not a test. What matters is steady, happy practice. If you have a real concern about development, ask a doctor or the teacher.
