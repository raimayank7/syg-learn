Preschool Games

When is a child ready for learning games?

Parents often wonder if their child is ready for learning games. There is no single age. Look for signs, not for a number. A child is ready for a simple game when they can point to things on request, tap a button on purpose, and stay with one activity for a couple of minutes.

Signs your child is ready

  • They point to a picture when you name it ("where is the dog?").
  • They can tap or touch on purpose, not just bang the screen.
  • They can sit with you for two or three minutes.
  • They enjoy naming things and being right.

Start with something real

A game works best after the child has met the idea in real life. Play with red and blue blocks before Colour Match. Count real steps before Count the Things. The game then feels like a favourite thing in a new form.

A good first week

  • Day 1: Colour Match level 1, one round, with you beside your child.
  • Day 2: Animal Sounds level 1. Make the noises yourself.
  • Day 3: no screen, a colour hunt around the house.
  • Day 4: Shape Spotter level 1.
  • Day 5: Big and Small level 1 and a real size game with shoes.
  • Day 6 and 7: rest from the screen; play outside, read a book.

How to sit with your child

  • Sit next to them, not behind or across.
  • Read the question aloud and let them tap.
  • Name what you see and what happens: "You found blue."
  • When they get one wrong, say "try another one" in a warm voice. The game does the same.
  • End while they still want more.

When to stop

Stop if the child is tired, hungry, upset or no longer looking at the screen. Another day will go better. A game should end with a smile, not with tears.

For how much screen time is right at each age, read our page on screen time for young children.

If your child is not interested

Many children are simply not interested in games at two or three, and that is fine. A child who prefers building blocks, singing or running has found a good way to learn. Wait a few months and try again, or skip games altogether. Nothing in these games is needed for school.

Playing with more than one child

If an older child plays too, let them take turns being the "reader" who reads each question aloud to the younger one. This helps both: the older one practises reading and patience, and the younger one hears the words. Do not let the older child take over the screen; tapping is half of the fun.

Keep a small routine

Children like to know what comes next. A short, regular play time, such as ten minutes after tea two or three days a week, is better than a long session once in a while. Say when it starts and when it ends, and keep to it.
