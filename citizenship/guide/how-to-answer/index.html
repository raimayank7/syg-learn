How to answer: short, exact, aloud

Knowing the facts is one skill. Saying an accepted answer clearly to an officer is another. These habits make the second one easy.

Choose one answer and keep it

Many questions list several accepted answers. For "Name one power of the president" the 2025 list accepts several. You need one. Learning all of them is wasted effort and makes you hesitate. For each question choose the answer that is shortest and easiest for you to pronounce, and always give that one.

Use the words of the official list

Both official lists carry the same sentence: USCIS is aware that there may be additional correct answers, but applicants are encouraged to respond using the answers provided. An answer of your own may be true and still cause doubt. The printed answers never do.

Reading the brackets

The lists print some words in round brackets, for example "(U.S.) Constitution" or "(Thomas) Jefferson". The usual reading is that the words in brackets may be said or left out: "Jefferson" alone and "Thomas Jefferson" are both shown as one answer. Text in square brackets is an instruction, not part of an answer, such as the note telling residents of Washington, D.C. how to answer the question about senators.

Give the number the question asks for

If the question says "Name two" or "What are three", stop when you have given that many. If you give fewer, the answer is incomplete. See the full list of such questions.

Keep answers short

"What is the supreme law of the land?" "The Constitution." That is a complete answer. Long explanations add chances to say something wrong and are not needed. If you like full sentences for English practice, keep them simple: "The Constitution is the supreme law of the land."

If you did not hear or understand

The policy manual says an applicant may ask for words to be repeated or rephrased. Practise the sentences: "Could you repeat the question, please?" and "Could you say it another way?" Asking is normal and far better than guessing at a question you did not catch.

Listen for the key word

Several questions sound alike. "Who signs bills?" and "Who vetoes bills?" share an answer, but "How many senators?" and "How many senators does each state have?" do not: 100 and 2. "Who does a senator represent?" and "Who elects senators?" are different questions too. When you practise, have your helper read the similar ones back to back.

Names and pronunciation

Practise aloud the names you plan to use: your senator, your representative, your governor, a tribe, a holiday. Say them slowly. A clear, slow answer is better than a fast one the officer cannot follow.

When your mind goes blank

Take a breath and ask for the question again. One wrong answer does not fail the test: in the 2025 version you may miss eight and still pass; in the 2008 version, four. Let a missed question go and listen to the next one.
