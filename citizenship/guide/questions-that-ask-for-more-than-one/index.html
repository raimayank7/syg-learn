Questions that ask for more than one answer

Most civics questions need one answer, even when the list shows many. A few ask for two, three or five. Give fewer and the answer is not complete.

We went through both official lists and pulled out every question whose wording asks for more than one item. There are 7 in the 2025 list and 6 in the 2008 list. Because there are so few, it is worth preparing each of them separately: decide now exactly which items you will say, and always say them in the same order.

A ready set for each

These are our suggestions for short sets that are easy to say. Any items from the accepted answers are equally right.

  • Two ideas from the Declaration and the Constitution (2025): equality and liberty.
  • Two rights in the Declaration (2008): life and liberty.
  • Two Cabinet-level positions: Secretary of State and Attorney General. Both appear in the accepted answers of both lists. (The lists differ on some other titles, so check yours.)
  • Rights of everyone living in the United States: freedom of speech, freedom of religion and freedom of assembly. Say the first two if your list asks for two.
  • Promises in the Oath of Allegiance: obey the laws of the United States, and defend the Constitution.
  • Civic participation: vote, and join a political party.
  • Original states: New York, New Jersey, Virginia, Georgia and Maryland. The 2008 list asks for three, the 2025 list for five.
  • National holidays: Independence Day, Thanksgiving and Christmas. The 2008 list asks for two, the 2025 list for three.

Check each item of your chosen set against the accepted answers in the tables above before you memorise it. Then practise the whole set as one phrase.

Questions that only sound like they need more

"Name the three branches of government" in the 2025 list is answered with one phrase: legislative, executive and judicial. "What are the two parts of the U.S. Congress?" is also one phrase: the Senate and House. Learn these as single answers.
