How to follow current affairs for exams: a method

This page is our own advice. It does not report any event, and it does not claim that any source is official for any exam. Check each exam's own notice for what it says about current affairs.

1. Decide what you are preparing for

Read the syllabus of your exam. Many syllabi say "current events of national and international importance" without listing topics, so you have to decide what counts. A good rule is to ask whether an item touches the polity, economy, geography, environment, science or history parts of the syllabus.

2. Choose few sources and keep them

Pick one daily newspaper and one weekly or monthly summary, and keep to them. Reading ten sources gives repetition, not knowledge. Prefer sources that name where an item came from, such as a ministry, a court or a report.

3. Read daily for a fixed short time

Thirty to forty-five minutes a day, at the same hour, beats a long weekend session. Read the headline items first, then the explainers.

4. Note one line per item

For each item write a date, one line of what happened, who was involved, and the syllabus area. Our notes keeper does this in your browser. Writing the line forces you to understand the item.

5. Learn the background, not only the headline

An item is easier to remember when you know what came before it: the law it amends, the earlier report, the previous host of a summit. Spend a minute finding the background of the three most important items of the day.

6. Revise weekly and monthly

Every week, reread the week's notes and cover them to test yourself. Every month, reread the month in one sitting and cross out what you can already recall. See the revision plan.

7. Use the days list as a calendar

International days link to themes that appear in the news around the day. When a day comes up, read about its theme that week. Our month pages list them.
