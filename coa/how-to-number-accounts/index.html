How to number a chart of accounts

Numbering is optional but useful: codes keep accounts in a sensible order, make lookups quick and let a spreadsheet sort the list. The scheme used on this site is our own convention, not an official standard, and you are free to change it.

The ranges we use

ClassFirst codeLast codeExample from the starter chart
Asset100019991000 Cash in hand
Liability200029992000 Trade payables (suppliers we owe)
Equity300039993000 Owner's capital
Income400049994000 Sales
Expense500069995000 Purchases

The first digit gives the class. Because the statements list assets, then liabilities and equity, then income and then expenses, sorting by code puts the accounts in the order you read them in.

Rules that keep it tidy

  1. Leave gaps. The lists step by 10 in many places, so you can add an account between two existing ones without renumbering.
  2. Put related accounts next to each other: a contra account sits right after the account it reduces (for example accumulated depreciation after furniture).
  3. Never reuse a code for a different account, even if you delete the old one. Old records would then be misread.
  4. Keep codes short: four digits is enough for a small business.

Adding your own

In the tool, choose a code between the codes around it, in the range of its class. Codes need not be numbers: the tool accepts letters too, and sorts numeric codes first. If you use accounting software, check what code format it accepts before you import.

These lists and the numbering are our own examples. They are not an official, statutory or board-approved chart, and they claim to follow no accounting standard or tax rule. Rules differ between places and organisations, so check what your own accountant, course, lender or governing body requires.
