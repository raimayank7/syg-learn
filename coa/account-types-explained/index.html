Account types explained: asset, liability, equity, income, expense

Every account in a chart of accounts belongs to one of five classes. The class tells you where the account appears in the final statements and which side (debit or credit) its balance normally sits on. Getting the class right is the most important decision when you add a new account.

The five classes

ClassWhat it holdsNormal balanceAppears inExamples from the starter chart
AssetThings the business owns or is owedDebitPosition statement (balance sheet)Cash in hand, Bank current account
LiabilityAmounts the business owes to othersCreditPosition statement (balance sheet)Trade payables (suppliers we owe), Accrued expenses
EquityThe owner's share: money put in, less money taken out, plus profits keptCreditPosition statement (balance sheet)Owner's capital, Profit and loss summary
IncomeMoney earned by the business's activityCreditProfit statementSales, Interest received
ExpenseCosts of running the businessDebitProfit statementPurchases, Freight inward

How to decide the class of a new account

  1. Ask: does the business own it or is it owed to the business? That is an asset.
  2. Does the business owe it to someone else, and must it be paid or delivered later? That is a liability.
  3. Is it the owner's money, or a fund the organisation holds? That is equity.
  4. Is it earned from customers or others for the business's activity? That is income.
  5. Is it used up in running the business? That is an expense.

Easy to confuse

  • Deposits: a deposit you paid is an asset; a deposit you hold for someone else is a liability, because you may have to return it.
  • Advances: money received before you supply goods is a liability until you supply them.
  • Drawings: money the owner takes out is not an expense of the business; it reduces the owner's equity.
  • Buying equipment: the cost is an asset, and each year's use of it is the expense called depreciation.

These lists and the numbering are our own examples. They are not an official, statutory or board-approved chart, and they claim to follow no accounting standard or tax rule. Rules differ between places and organisations, so check what your own accountant, course, lender or governing body requires.
