How do scientists date rocks and fossils?

A fossil does not come with a date written on it. Scientists find its age from the rock around it.

Idea 1: layers

Rock often forms in layers, like a stack of pancakes. Normally the bottom layer was made first, so layers lower down are older and layers higher up are younger. A fossil in a layer is usually about as old as the layer. Sometimes the rock has been folded or tipped by movements of the Earth, so scientists check carefully.

Idea 2: matching fossils

Some animals lived for a short time and spread widely. If the same kind of fossil is found in two places, the two layers may be about the same age. Scientists use such fossils as markers.

Idea 3: atomic clocks

Some kinds of atoms are unstable and slowly change into other kinds at a steady, known speed. The time for half of them to change is called the half-life. By measuring how much has changed in a mineral, scientists can work out how long ago the mineral formed. This works best on rocks that formed from hot melted rock, such as layers of volcanic ash. If a fossil lies between an older ash layer and a younger one, its age must be between the two dates.

Why a database gives a range

The dates have some uncertainty, and a fossil is found in a layer that covers a span of time. So the Paleobiology Database gives, for each genus, an oldest possible and a youngest possible age for the first and the last time it appears. On the dinosaur pages you will see "between 83.6 and 72.2 million years ago" style ranges. They mean "the fossils say it appears somewhere in this window". The ages are revised as scientists measure more.
