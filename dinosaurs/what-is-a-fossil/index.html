What is a fossil?

A fossil is a sign of ancient life that has been kept in rock. Scientists often say that a fossil is older than about 10,000 years.

What can be a fossil?

  • Bones and teeth, which can slowly be filled and replaced by minerals until they are stone.
  • Shells and the hard parts of plants.
  • Footprints, burrows and other marks that an animal left in mud that later hardened.
  • Eggs, and even droppings.

How does a bone become a fossil?

Imagine an animal that dies near a river. If its body is covered quickly by mud or sand, the soft parts can rot away while the hard parts are buried. Over a very long time the layers above get heavier and the mud turns to rock. Water that seeps through carries minerals, which can fill tiny spaces in the bone. Slowly the bone is changed into stone that keeps its shape.

Why are fossils rare?

Most animals never become fossils. Most bodies are eaten, rot, or are broken up before they can be buried. Then the rock has to survive for millions of years, and someone has to find it. This is why a fossil record is a patchy record, and why the number of fossils found of a dinosaur is not the number of dinosaurs that lived.

What a fossil can tell us

A fossil can tell us the shape of a bone or tooth, where it was found and in which layer of rock. From that, scientists work out how the animal was built and when it lived. They cannot see skin, sounds or behaviour in a bone, so those are mostly unknown. When a page on this site says "not given in the data", that is because nobody has recorded it.
