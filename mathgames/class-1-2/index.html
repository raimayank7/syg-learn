Maths games for classes 1 and 2

11 games that suit classes 1 and 2. Tap one to play.

In the first two classes children are building the ground floor of arithmetic: counting with meaning, the number pairs that make ten, place value up to a hundred, simple adding and taking away, reading a clock and counting money. Nothing learnt later stands firm without these.

Games suit this age well. A six-year-old will happily do forty small sums in a game and resist ten on a page. Keep sessions to about ten minutes, sit with the child, and talk about how they got each answer. The talk matters more than the score.

Games made for this stage

  • Count the DotsDots appear in no fixed order. Count them and tap the right number.Nursery to Class 1
  • Make TenTwelve number tiles. Tap two that add up to the target. Clear the whole board.Classes 1 to 3
  • Bigger or SmallerTwo numbers side by side. Tap the bigger one, or the smaller one when asked.Classes 1 to 7
  • Clock TimeA clock face shows a time. Choose the time it shows.Classes 1 to 4
  • Coin CountCoins and notes are laid out. Add them up and choose the total.Classes 1 to 4

Also suitable

These games have levels that fit classes 1 and 2 too.

  • Sum SprintQuick sums against yourself. Type the answer on the number pad.Classes 1 to 7
  • Missing SignA sum with its sign missing. Which of +, −, × and ÷ makes it true?Classes 2 to 5
  • Place ValueWhich digit is in the hundreds place? What is the 7 really worth?Classes 2 to 5
  • What Comes NextA row of numbers follows a rule. Find the rule and choose the next number.Classes 2 to 7
  • Memory MatchTwelve cards face down. Turn over two at a time and match each sum with its answer.Classes 1 to 5
  • Odd One OutThree numbers share a property and one does not. Find the one that does not belong.Classes 2 to 7

What to look for at this age

  • Counting on, not counting all. To add 6 and 3, a child should start from 6 and count 7, 8, 9, not count out both piles from one.
  • Number pairs to ten known by heart: 1 and 9, 2 and 8, 3 and 7, 4 and 6, 5 and 5.
  • Tens and ones. 34 is three tens and four ones, not a 3 next to a 4.
  • Reading the hour and the half hour on a clock with hands.

If a game is too hard

Go down a level, or leave that game for a month. Struggle that ends in success builds confidence, but repeated failure teaches a child that "I am bad at maths", which is rarely true and hard to undo. At this age real objects help more than any screen: count stairs, share out biscuits, lay the table for five.
